ABSTRACT
Systolic array designs are gaining popularity due to their applica-
tions in hardware acceleration for ML computing, such as CNNs
and transformers. Increasingly large ML models necessitate very
high circuit energy-efficiency, which is highly correlated with min-
imizing placement wirelength in chip physical design. However,
existing placement techniques are mostly general purpose and
overlook unique properties of systolic array designs. We propose a
mixed-size placement approach, called SysMix, which is tailored for
systolic arrays and leverage their partial regularity in hierarchical
design methodologies. Experimental results from multiple CNN de-
signs show that SysMix achieves53% wirelength reduction and 15X
speedup compared to a commercial placer and a state-of-the-art
academic placer.

1 INTRODUCTION
Systolic array, composed of an array of PEs (Processing Elements),
serves as a cornerstone for massively parallel computing. Systolic
arrays are popular in hardware acceleration for ML (Machine Learn-
ing) computing, such as CNNs (Convolutional Neural Networks)
and transformers, and have been adopted in industry, e.g., Google
TPU [11]. As shown in Figure 1, systolic arrays are characterized
by their highly regular structure, coupled with interconnect domi-
nated by local connections, i.e., connections between adjacent PEs
as opposed to non-adjacent PEs. Intuitively, such a regular structure
is very friendly to layout designs and can potentially facilitate im-
proved layout solution quality. Since all PEs in a systolic array are

Such macro IP-based design reuse is commonly seen in hierarchi-

To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first study on
mixed-size placement tailored for systolic array designs on ASICs
(Application-Specific Integrated Circuits) by leveraging their regu-
larity. Although mixed-size placement has been extensively studied
in the past [3, 6, 15, 1, 4], they are mostly general-purpose and pay
almost no attention to design regularities. Design regularity is con-
sidered in datapath placement [7, 20]. In addition to the difference
between systolic array’s 2D regularity and conventional datapath’s
1D regularity, dust cells exist between PEs of a systolic array while
datapath cells form contiguous rows without random logic cells
in the middle. Therefore, datapath placement techniques are not
directly applicable to systolic array designs. A macro placement
approach for TPU designs is introduced in [19]. It is a reinforcement
learning approach with little emphasis on exploiting the design
regularity. Systolic array placement is also studied for FPGAs (Field-
Programmable Gate Arrays) [14, 10].
Although the work of [9] is for placement of systolic array de-
signs, it treats all PE cells as dust cells and solves a cell placement
problem instead of mixed-size placement. Thus, it is for flat designs
as opposed to hierarchical designs as in our work. Both flat design
and hierarchical design are popular methodologies, and each has
its own advantages and disadvantages. In a hierarchical design
methodology, where the PE design is purchased from an IP vendor,
it makes little business sense to flatten the PE design and use cell
placement like [9]. Likewise, in a flat design methodology, it is
usually better to apply cell placement like [9] than macro-based
mixed-size placement like our work. Therefore, the work of [9] and

The exploitation of structural regularity in placement of systolic
array designs is not as straightforward as it appears to be. Due
to the PE connections with global buffer, control logic cells, and
IO pins, placing PEs in a strictly regular array usually results in
increased wirelength. Instead, small wirelength results often exhibit
partial regularity, where the spatial orders among PEs generally
follow the schematic but there is not necessarily alignment for
rows and columns. To solve the problem of mixed-size placement
with partial regularity, we propose SysMix, an approach with three
key ingredients: (1) PE-driven dust cell clustering; (2) regularity-
aware macro/PE placement via LP (Linear Programming); (3) dust
cell placement with a new initial placement technique. In SysMix,
the regularity is exploited to reduce solution search space and
help achieve better solution quality. The benefits of exploiting the
regularity are clearly observed in the experimental results. The
contributions of this work are summarized as follows.
• This is the first study on exploiting regularity for mixed-size
placement of systolic arrays in hierarchical designs, to the
best of our knowledge.
• A dust cell clustering technique is proposed for reserving
the right amount of space in PE placement.
• A linear programming-based macro placement technique
that makes use of partial regularity is developed.
• A dust cell placement technique with customized initial
placement is proposed.
• SysMix is compared with 5 other methods on multiple CNN
designs. Only SysMix, a commercial tool and another state-
of-the-art academic placer successfully completed all cases.
SysMix achieves 53% wirelength reduction and 15× speedup
compared to the commercial tool and the academic placer.
2 PREVIOUS WORKS
Mixed-size Placement. NTUplace3 [3] is an early influen-
tial work on mixed-size placement based on multi-level analyt-
ical techniques. RePlAce [6] is an electrostatics-based analytical
placer, which outperforms most of its prior works on public domain
benchmark suites and forms the key basis for many recent plac-
ers. DREAMPlace [15] is a very fast analytical placer leveraging
neural network training infrastructure and GPUs. AutoDMP [1]
is a mixed-size placement framework based on DREAMPlace. It
uses multi-objective Bayesian optimization to fine-tune the param-
eters of the DREAMPlace engine, and achieves PPA (Performance,
Power, Area) comparable to commercial tools. The work of [4] pro-
poses a stronger mixed-size global placement backbone based on
DREAMPlace. It archives better robustness for convergence, and
better HPWL (Half-Perimeter Wire-Length) and PPA.
Macro Placement. Macro placement has been extensively stud-
ied, and we summarize some recent research results in this area.
MDP-trees [16] is a simulated annealing-based macro placement ap-
proach, where the MDP (Multi-Domain Packing) tree is a new solu-
tion representation technique. A deep reinforcement learning-based
macro placement approach is presented in [19] and has been applied
in Google TPU designs. RTL-MP [12] is a simulated annealing-based
macro placer that exploits RTL information regarding logical hier-
archy. It achieves equally good or better PPA than a commercial
tool and human experts, but suffers from scalability in runtime. A
comparative study on different macro placement and mixed-size
placement methods is provided in [5]. The results show that routed
wirelength, area, and power are often highly correlated. AutoDMP
and a commercial tool often achieve the best results in this study.
Datapath Placement. In [7], datapath cells are first clustered
into a single macro and then a mixed-size placement is performed.
Next, the datapath macro is declustered and datapath cells are
placed while the random logic cells are fixed. It concludes with
a random logic placement fine-tuning stage. The work of [20] si-
multaneously places datapath cells and random logic cells through
a force-directed algorithm. Pseudo nets are inserted to align cells
across multiple-bit slices to address datapath regularity.
Systolic Array Design Placement. In [14], a simulated
annealing-based FPGA placement technique is developed consid-
ering the 2D regularity of systolic arrays. It achieves a 2X to 28X
speedup compared to VPR [17], a well-known FPGA layout tool,
with limited circuit performance loss. The work of [9] proposes a
global placement framework using soft 2D regularity constraints
for systolic array-based CNN designs. It places PE cells instead of
PE macros in flat design methodologies. Compared with RePlAce,
it reduces HPWL by 6%. Since flat designs have fewer constraints
than hierarchical designs, they may lead to better solution quality
at the expense of longer turn-around time. Although it is also a
systolic array placer, comparing a mixed-size placement and a cell
placement of the same circuit would mostly disclose the difference
between a hierarchical design methodology versus a flat design
methodology instead of the effectiveness of placement algorithms.
A new algorithmic approach for systolic array placement on FPGAs
is proposed in [10]. Compared with other FPGA placement tools, it
obtains a 24% reduction in HPWL.
Summary. There is almost no prior study on mixed-size place-
ment for systolic array designs in ASICs by leveraging their regu-
larity.
3 BACKGROUND
3.1 Graph Clustering by Bubble-FOS/C
We make use of Bubble-FOS/C [18], a graph partitioning/clustering
algorithm, in our mixed-size placement. It is a connectivity-driven
approach rather than minimizing cut sizes. Its framework has three
stages: (1) randomly choosing a certain number of nodes as seeds;
(2) growing each cluster from a seed node to its neighboring nodes
by simulating fluid diffusion till all nodes are clustered; (3) for each
cluster, finding its center as the new seed and regrowing from
the new seeds to form new clusters. In our work, only stage 2 is
needed as the seeds are PEs, which do not need to be randomly
selected at the beginning or reselected after stage 2. Stage 2 is
an iterative process. In each iteration, a certain amount of fluid
moves from node 𝑣 𝑗 to another 𝑣𝑖 with probability 𝐴𝑖,𝑗 if there is
an edge (𝑣𝑖, 𝑣 𝑗 ) in the given graph. The probability depends on the
connectivity between 𝑣𝑖 and 𝑣 𝑗 . At the end, a node is assigned to a
cluster if its fluid mostly comes from the seed of this cluster. This
iterative simulation of diffusion behaves like a Markov chain, and

4 PROBLEM FORMULATION
The input is a systolic array-based design characterized by a hyper-
graph 𝐺 = (𝑉 , 𝐸). The node set 𝑉 is composed of a set of PEs 𝑃, a
set of dust cells𝐶 and a set of fixed pins𝑇 corresponding to IOs and
preplaced global buffer, i.e.,𝑉 = 𝑃 ∪ 𝐶 ∪ 𝑇 . Set 𝑃 is an 𝑀 × 𝐾 array
of PEs, where 𝑀 and 𝐾 are natural numbers. The hyperedge set𝐸 is
the netlist indicating connections among PEs, dust cells, and fixed
pins. Each PE is treated as a hard macro, and its internal nets are
neglected. The mixed-size placement is to decide PE and dust cell
locations in a 2D plane such that the total HPWL (Half Perimeter
Wire-Length) is minimized and there is no overlap among any PEs,
dust cells, and fixed pins. Mixed-size placement for systolic array
designs exploiting regularity is a relatively new problem. As such,
it will be tackled in a step-by-step manner with an emphasis on
HPWL in this work like many other mixed-size placement works.
Other objectives, such as routability, will be addressed in our future
research.
5 THE PROPOSED SYSMIX PLACEMENT
5.1 Overview
Although the structure of a systolic array is very regular, its place-
ment is not straightforward for two reasons. First, there are wire
connections between PEs and fixed pins, which may distort the
regularity. Second, there are a significant number of dust cells in-
terleaved between PEs, which also disturb the regularity. The effect
of fixed pins and dust cells on regularity is demonstrated in [9].
Such partial regularity is significantly more difficult to exploit than
complete regularity. A similar difficulty is also observed in datapath
placement [20]. On the other hand, even partial regularity provides
an opportunity for further HPWL reduction.
Figure 2: Overview of the proposed SysMix placement
method, where the green parts are our contributions.
An overview of our proposed SysMix placement methodology
is provided in Figure 2. In the phase of macro placement, PEs are
placed with the consideration of dust cells through dust cell clus-
tering. The macro placement is realized through LP. In the second
phase, dust cells are placed while the PEs are fixed. Both the macro
placement and dust cell placement leverage the partial regularity
of systolic array designs.
5.2 Macro Placement
5.2.1 Dust Cell Clustering for C3-Macro Generation. A C3-macro
consists of a PE surrounded by a subset of related dust cells, like a
Chocolate-Covered Cherry. The PE is a hard macro (cherry) and
its surrounding dust cells form a firm shell (chocolate). In this step,
every dust cell is clustered to a certain PE to form an𝑀 × 𝐾 array of
C3-macros. Such C3-macros provide a rough estimate of how much
space to be reserved for dust cells around each PE when deciding
PE locations.
This is a special graph clustering problem different from ordinary
graph clustering/partitioning [2, 13]. Thus, we adopt the Bubble-
FOS/C algorithm [18], which fits our need very well. The overall
Bubble-FOS/C algorithm is briefly reviewed in Section 3.1. Since
we can directly use PEs as cluster seeds and there is no need for
further seed adjustment, only stage 2 of Bubble-FOS/C is employed
here.
Starting from PEs as cluster seeds, dust cells are clustered to PEs
by simulating a fluid diffusion process. As illustrated in Figure 3,

5.3 Dust Cell Placement
In this phase, dust cells are declustered from C3-macros and placed
while the PE locations are fixed. Our dust cell placement includes
three steps: (1) initial placement; (2) global placement; (3) detailed
placement and legalization. The initial placement in our SysMix is
a new technique that makes use of the regularity of systolic arrays
and will be elaborated later in this section. The global placement
is carried out using RePlAce [6] with a change. Although PE areas
intuitively need to be counted toward cell densities in global place-
ment, their large sizes overshadow the density of dust cells, which
are to be placed in this phase. In order to let RePlAce focus on dust
cells instead of PEs, we scale PE dimensions with a factor the same
as the target density throughout our execution of RePlAce-based
dust cell placement. Step 3, detailed placement and legalization, is
realized by calling NTUplace3 [3].
In conventional cell placement methods [3, 6], initial solutions
are generated randomly or through quadratic placement. In our
SysMix, we propose a new initial solution technique for dust cells
by leveraging the regularity of systolic array designs. The main
idea is to use the PE placement as a backbone and then place dust
cells using the results of clustering described in Section 5.2.1.
The initial placement is based on dust cell connections with PEs.
For simplicity, we use cells to refer dust cells. The netlist connections
are described by matrix 𝐴, which is described in Section 5.2.1. It is
an 𝑁 × 𝑁 matrix, where its upper-left |𝐶 | × |𝐶 | sub-matrix describes
connections among cells, its lower-right |𝑃 | × | 𝑃 | sub-matrix is for
connections among PEs, the upper-right |𝐶 | × | 𝑃 | sub-matrix and
lower-left |𝑃 | × | 𝐶 | sub-matrix indicate connections between cells
and PEs. We derive a |𝐶 | × | 𝑃 | matrix Φ from 𝐴 to capture direct
and indirect connections between a cell and a PE. There is a direct
connection between a cell 𝑣𝑖 ∈ 𝐶 and a PE 𝑣 𝑗 ∈ 𝑃 if there is an edge
𝑒 ∈ 𝐸 such that 𝑣𝑖 ∈ 𝑒 and 𝑣 𝑗 ∈ 𝑒. There is an indirect connection
between a cell 𝑣𝑖 ∈ 𝐶 and a PE 𝑣 𝑗 ∈ 𝑃 if there is another cell
𝑣𝑘 ∈ 𝐶 such that {𝑣𝑖, 𝑣𝑘 } ⊆ 𝑒 ∈ 𝐸 and 𝑣𝑘 is assigned to C3-macro

6 EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
6.1 Experiment Setup
Several systolic array-based CNN circuit designs are obtained ac-
cording to [21] for ASIC style. These circuits are synthesized using
Synopsys Design Compiler with the ASAP7 cell library [8]. They
cover different dataflow types, WS (Weight Stationary) and OS
(Output Stationary), and various PE array sizes. The characteristics
of these circuits are summarized in the first column of Table 1.
We compared our method (SysMix) with the following state-of-
the-art mixed-size placers
• RePlAce[6];
• DREAMPlace[15];
• AutoDMP[1];
• The work of [4];
• A commercial placement tool.
AutoDMP usually generates multiple solutions with different PPA
(Performance Power Area) tradeoffs. We pick its minimum HPWL
solution for a fair comparison. Except AutoDMP and [4], all the
other methods are evaluated on an 8-thread CPU machine with
3.4GHz frequency and 250GB RAM. AutoDMP can only be per-
formed on a GPU platform, and it runs on NVIDIA GeForce RTX
2080 Ti in this experiment. The result of [4] was obtained from an
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 GPU.
6.2 Main Results
The main results of HPWL and total runtime are summarized in
Table 1. All the HPWL values except those labeled with illegal
are legalized results. For those results labeled with illegal, global
placement was successful, and their global placement HPWL results
are reported if available. Please note that each tool has its own
legalizer, e.g., the legalization of DREAMPlace was performed using
DREAMPlace’s legalizer. The global placement of RePlAce cannot
converge for circuit WS8 and circuit WS64.
Among the six methods being compared, only our SysMix, the
commercial tool, and AutoDMP can successfully find legalized so-
lutions for all circuits. Compared to the commercial tool and Au-
toDMP, our SysMix can reduce wirelength by over 53% on average
and are 15× - 217× faster. Although DREAMPlace’s results look
close to ours, DREAMPlace failed to legalize five out of the eight
circuits. Among all methods, RePlAce is the fastest, but it failed in
three circuits, and the available HPWL results are 78% larger than
our SysMix on average. The method of [4] failed in legalizing WS32
and generated 2.7× HPWL compared to our SysMix.
6.3 Ablation Studies
6.3.1 Effectiveness of LP-Based C3-Macro Placement. We compared
our SysMix with two alternative approaches, which perform the
PE placement differently.
(1) Regular PE placement. PEs instead of C3-macros are placed in
a regular 2D array, where rows and columns are well aligned.
Then, dust cells are placed using RePlAce with the PEs are
fixed. This approach is straightforward yet the tricky part is
the spacing among adjacement PEs. Since this is a regular
array, the spacing for different columns and rows are the
same. Too small spacing may cause legalization failures while
too large spacing increases HPWL unnecessarily. For each
case, we conduct multiple runs and choose the minimum
spacing that allows successful legalization.
(2) RePlAce with regular initial PE placement (RePlAce-RI). This
is almost the same as RePlAce except that the initial PE place-
ment is a regular 2D array instead of a random placement.

6.3.5 Effectiveness of PE Scaling in Dust Cell Global Placement.
During the dust cell global placement in SysMix, we scale the di-
mensions of each PE by a factor equal to the target density such
that PEs do not overshadow dust cells in bin density estimation of
RePlAce [6]. Despite its simplicity, this PE scaling technique plays
a critical role in the success of SysMix. When PEs are not scaled as
such, the global placement fails to converge for both WS8 and OS8,
the two smallest cases.
7 CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH
This work provides the first study on mixed-size placement for
systolic array designs in hierarchical ASIC style, to the best of
our knowledge. The proposed approach SysMix makes use of par-
tial regularity in systolic array designs and produces significantly
better wirelength results compared to state-of-the-art techniques
including a commercial tool, which either fail in obtaining legal-
ized solutions or lead to considerably greater wirelength. Ablation
studies have been performed to demonstrate the effectiveness of
each component in our SysMix placer. In future research, we will
extend this work to handle routability and evaluate solutions with
global routing.
